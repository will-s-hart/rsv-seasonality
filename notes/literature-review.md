# Literature review: RSV, climate, and epidemic timing

Working synthesis, assessed **18 November 2026**. This is a targeted survey of primary studies, not a systematic review. Priority: identify transmission mechanisms that could transfer between Japan and the UK. See [data sources](data-sources.md), [modelling methodology](modelling-methodology.md), and [analysis plan](analysis-plan.md).

## Starting point and research opportunity

[Park et al. (2025), *Interplay between climate and childhood mixing can explain a sudden shift in RSV seasonality in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-66184-y) is the central benchmark. Their SIRS model estimates flexible periodic transmission and pandemic perturbations; subsequent regressions relate transmission to weekly climate climatologies. Four islands show semiannual transmission peaks, with low transmission at intermediate humidity/temperature; Hokkaido differs. Simulations show that increased susceptibility **or** transmissibility can advance epidemic timing. Increased childcare attendance is a proposed explanation, not an established cause. Temperature and specific humidity correlate above 0.96, preventing attribution to either independently; pooled relationships also fit Ryukyu poorly. In joint regressions, humidity curvature is significant in Honshu/Ryukyu but not Kyushu/Shikoku. These qualifications matter for a universal curve. [Published full-text mirror](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12738541/).

The authors' [public reproduction repository](https://github.com/parksw3/perturbation) contains R/Stan models, epidemiological/demographic data, processing scripts, and manuscript sources. Start with reproduction in Julia. The March 2025 preprint has a different title; use the final publication for scientific claims.

**Proposed contribution:** estimate climate responses jointly with renewal dynamics and observation processes, test transfer across climates and years, and establish whether additional response flexibility is identifiable and useful. Discovery of two seasonal transmission peaks or a U-shaped association alone is already well precedented.

## Evidence to build on

| Primary study | Evidence and method | Consequence for this project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Pitzer et al. (2015), *Environmental drivers of RSV in the United States*](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.ppat.1004591) | Age-stratified repeat-infection SIRS model fitted to hospital and laboratory data. Identical annual forcing can generate alternating large/early and small/late epidemics. Seasonal forcing parameters correlate with climate, but weekly weather does not explain all year-to-year variation. | Retain susceptibility/reinfection dynamics; epidemic periodicity is not a direct measurement of forcing periodicity. Include a calendar-only mechanistic baseline. |
| [Shobugawa et al. (2017; online 2016), *Occurrence of RSV in summer in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095026881600220X) | National sentinel data, 2007–2014; summer-restricted negative-binomial regression and decomposition by climate division. Hot, humid conditions interact positively; summer circulation occurs particularly in southwestern divisions. | Analyse prefectures or coherent regions, rather than treating Japan as homogeneous. Their 28.2°C interaction threshold is study-specific observational evidence, not a biological constant. |
| [Baker et al. (2019), *Epidemic dynamics of RSV in current and future climates*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-13562-y) | US county/Mexican state data with nonlinear epidemic models and statistical analyses. Transmission decreases with specific humidity and increases with rainfall. Omitting precipitation produces a U-shaped humidity association, with a fitted minimum around 11.6 g/kg. | Precipitation is a mandatory competing explanation for the apparent high-humidity branch. Compare humidity-only, temperature-only, and joint weather specifications before interpreting maxima. |
| [*Meteorological drivers of RSV infections in Singapore* (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-76888-4) | Daily effective reproduction numbers inferred from hospitalisations, followed by lagged weather regression. Weather explains an additional 15% of transmissibility variance beyond the baseline; precipitation/relative humidity associate positively. | A direct precedent for renewal-based climate analysis. Inference on effective reproduction numbers still includes susceptible depletion; propagate uncertainty rather than regressing on point estimates alone. |
| [Li et al. (2022), *RSV seasonality and meteorological factors in 13 European countries*](https://doi.org/10.2807/1560-7917.ES.2022.27.16.2100619) | Weekly surveillance, 2010–2019; weather-based local regression, including a held-out 2018/19 season. Lower temperature and higher relative humidity associate with activity; country-specific onset conditions vary. | Useful European comparison and forecasting benchmark. Activity associations do not identify an intrinsic transmission response; RH and specific humidity must not be treated as interchangeable. |
| [Eden et al. (2022), *Off-season RSV epidemics in Australia after easing COVID-19 restrictions*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-30485-3) | Laboratory surveillance, hospitalisation data, and viral genomes document substantial spring/summer resurgence following restrictions and a collapse in viral diversity. | Summer outbreaks can follow changed contacts, susceptibility, and introductions. Analyse pandemic seasons separately before asking whether climate alone accounts for summer transmission. |
| [Wagatsuma et al. (2023), *Nonlinear and multidelayed effects of meteorological drivers in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.3390/v15091914) | Weekly data from 47 prefectures, 2014–2019; GLMs with distributed-lag nonlinear models. Higher temperature and RH associate with greater incidence over a two-week cumulative window. | A practical non-neural statistical benchmark; short lags must be distinguished from delays between infection, symptoms, testing, and reporting. |
| [Liang et al. (2025), *Environmental conditions and RSV infection in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofaf392) | Japan, 47 prefectures, 2013–2019; hierarchical distributed-lag nonlinear models and Gaussian Process Boosting. Elevated activity associates with both low/high temperatures and low/high RH. | Multivariate nonlinear/ML associations already exist. The stronger contribution is joint dynamical inference and transportability, rather than substituting another flexible predictor. |
| [Wagatsuma (2026), *Spatial heterogeneity in RSV burden attributable to low/high temperature in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03751-x) | Weekly 2006–2019 data from all 47 prefectures; time-stratified case-crossover quasi-Poisson models and meta-analysis yield a pooled U-shaped temperature–incidence relationship. | Recent corroboration of nonlinearity, but incidence-based attributable fractions depend on modelling assumptions and do not establish a shared climate–transmission curve. |

## Interpretation and methodological implications

1. **Separate three objects.** A climate–transmission response, the annual path through climate space, and the resulting incidence curve are different. A U-shaped response has high values at the observed range's ends; this does not establish two interior local maxima. Annual transmission can have two peaks while incidence has one because susceptibility and epidemic history select which peak generates an outbreak.
2. **Test a surface, not merely two marginal curves.** Temperature, moisture, rainfall, school calendars, indoor exposure, and annual phase can covary. A neural network cannot create independent information absent from the data. Use within-location weather anomalies, diverse locations, and held-out seasons to interrogate separability. Display the climate support underlying every inferred surface.
3. **Keep alternative mechanisms explicit.** Compare shared climate response versus location-specific deviations; monotone humidity plus rainfall versus non-monotone humidity; and fixed climate response plus changing contacts/immunity versus changing climate response. Changes in testing and population age structure can also alter observed epidemic timing.
4. **Treat curve uncertainty as an outcome.** [Schmid et al. (2025), *Uncertainty quantification in UDEs*](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2024.0444) compare ensembles, variational inference, and MCMC on synthetic systems, including partially observed epidemic models. Good trajectory recovery can coexist with uncertain transmission functions, and ensemble selection affects bands. For this project, simulation recovery and calibrated function/prediction uncertainty should precede substantive claims about maxima.

## First reading and reproduction order

Read Park → Baker → Pitzer; inspect Park's data/code and reproduce its seasonal forcing comparison. Then use the Japan distributed-lag/GPBoost studies and the European/Singapore analyses as empirical benchmarks. Prioritise the question **“does a shared, uncertainty-aware climate response improve prediction and explain timing beyond calendar forcing and susceptibility?”** The UK–Japan explanation and mechanistic interpretation should depend on the answer, rather than being imposed as a model constraint.

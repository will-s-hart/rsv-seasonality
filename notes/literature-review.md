# Literature review: climate, behaviour, and RSV seasonality

Working synthesis, assessed **18 November 2026**. Targeted primary literature survey, not a systematic review. The project is a **multi-country renewal model with a neural climate–transmission component**, including Japan and a winter-dominant comparator from the outset. Country selection and surveillance compatibility are addressed in [data sources](data-sources.md); see also the [methodology](modelling-methodology.md) and [analysis plan](analysis-plan.md).

## Central hypothesis and evidential standard

The motivating pathway is **weather → changes in indoor time, contacts, crowding, ventilation, and heating/air-conditioning use → transmission**. Both cold conditions and hot/humid conditions could increase transmission opportunities through different behavioural responses. This is a hypothesis to investigate, not a demonstrated explanation for Japan's summer RSV activity. Direct effects on viral survival and host susceptibility remain possible alongside behaviour.

Behaviour should therefore help explain the climate response, rather than automatically be adjusted away as a nuisance. An outdoor climate–transmission curve estimated without measured mediators represents their combined association. It cannot by itself separate biological from behavioural effects. Behavioural responses and any temperature/moisture transitions can vary with adaptation, buildings, air-conditioning access, age, work, and local routines.

## RSV evidence and what remains unresolved

[Park et al. (2025), *Interplay between climate and childhood mixing can explain a sudden shift in RSV seasonality in Japan*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-66184-y) provides a relevant scientific benchmark. Its SIRS inference estimates flexible seasonal transmission, followed by climate regressions. Four islands show semiannual transmission peaks and lower transmission at intermediate humidity/temperature; Hokkaido differs. Increased susceptibility **or** transmissibility can advance epidemic timing in simulations. Increased childcare attendance is a proposed explanation, not an established cause. Temperature and specific humidity correlate above 0.96, preventing independent attribution; pooled curves also fit Ryukyu poorly. Joint humidity curvature is significant in Honshu/Ryukyu but not Kyushu/Shikoku. This supports investigating climate and mixing together while testing regional heterogeneity. It does not require adopting that paper's model. [Published full-text mirror](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12738541/).

| [Shobugawa et al. (2017; online 2016), summer RSV in Japan](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095026881600220X) | Sentinel observations, 2007–2014; summer cases concentrate in southwestern climate divisions and associate with interacting high temperature/RH. | Japan is heterogeneous. ## Primary evidence for the behavioural pathway

These studies measure behaviour or indoor conditions rather than RSV transmission. They support particular links in the proposed pathway; its complete mediation remains to be tested.

| Primary study | What was measured and found | Boundary on interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Willem et al. (2012), Belgium weather/contact diaries](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0048695) | Contacts linked to local weather: day type dominates, but long-duration contacts increase on cold/dry regular workdays. | School/work calendars matter; evidence does not establish that all cold-weather contact increases occur indoors or cause RSV. |
| [Graff Zivin & Neidell (2014), US time allocation](https://doi.org/10.1086/671766) | American Time Use Survey and within-county weather variation: warming from cold conditions increases outdoor leisure; high heat shifts nonemployed leisure indoors. Adaptation is also evident. | Supports both ends of a potential indoor-time response; additional indoor leisure need not mean more contacts or crowding. |
| [Chan et al. (2015; online 2014), Taiwan diaries](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0950268814000843) | Weather-linked diaries: high temperature associates with more medium-duration contacts; rain and humidity associations vary by contact/day type. | Behaviour can differ from temperate settings. A simple universal “bad weather increases contacts” rule is inadequate. |
| [Kummer et al. (2024), Shanghai seasonal contacts](https://doi.org/10.1111/irv.13301) | 965 diaries, December–May: higher seasonal temperature and warmer daily deviations associate with fewer contacts after covariate adjustment. | Separates weather anomalies from seasonal trend, but does not observe the full summer/hot branch or prove RSV mediation. |
| [Ou et al. (2025), temperature/humidity and Chinese social contacts](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mran.2025.100354) | Multi-city contact datasets: nonlinear associations include U-shaped total/nonphysical contacts and a joint temperature–RH association. | Relevant emerging observational evidence; survey mix, contact definition, and seasonal coverage require scrutiny before transferring the response. |
| [Jeong et al. (2022), Brisbane windows/air conditioning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2022.108953) | One year of continuous observations in 41 households: window use and AC operation are interdependent; window-open probability decreases beyond roughly 27°C in this sample. | Direct evidence for a building-operation pathway. The estimated transition is local, and window state is not measured ventilation rate or infection risk. |
| [Pan et al. (2021), indoor/outdoor climate in seven tropical cities](https://doi.org/10.1111/ina.12876) | Year-long measurements show outdoor moisture can represent indoor moisture better with AC off; AC and wet seasons weaken some indoor/outdoor relationships. | Outdoor weather is an imperfect exposure proxy, with building operation modifying the mapping. This study does not estimate contact or RSV effects. |

## Implications for this collaboration

1. **Make country diversity part of identification.** Fit Japan and a winter comparator jointly; add settings with contrasting moisture/rainfall and building practices when feasible. Test a shared response with modest regional deviations, rather than assuming universality or claiming independent temperature/humidity effects from collinear data.
2. **Treat behaviour as a substantive mechanism.** Seek contact diaries, time-use measures, occupancy/crowding, ventilation/building-operation proxies, childcare attendance, and AC/heating prevalence. Match age, setting, and geography where possible; general mobility is not equivalent to children's close contacts.
3. **Distinguish total climate association from mediation.** First estimate the renewal model's climate-associated transmission multiplier. Where compatible behavioural observations exist, assess whether predicted behaviour and building-operation differences explain regional response variation. Avoid claiming mediated causal fractions from RSV/weather series alone or learning separate unmeasured biological and behavioural functions without identifying information.
4. **Test alternatives with the same backbone.** Compare calendar forcing, simple climate curves, smooths, and a small neural response; challenge the humid branch with rainfall and calendar effects. Simulation recovery, temporal/geographical holdouts, and calibrated function uncertainty are necessary before interpreting curve shape.
5. **Describe maxima precisely.** A U-shaped response has elevated ends, not necessarily two interior maxima. Two peaks in annual transmission along a climate trajectory need not produce two incidence peaks: susceptibility, contact patterns, and introductions determine which opportunities become outbreaks.

The strongest opportunity is a multi-country, uncertainty-aware account of how climate-associated behaviour and transmission jointly relate to contrasting RSV seasonal regimes. Existing evidence makes this plausible; it leaves the relative pathways and the transferability of their response curves open.
